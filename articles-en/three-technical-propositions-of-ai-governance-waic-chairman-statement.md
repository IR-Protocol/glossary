# Parallel Reflections: Three Technical Propositions of AI Governance, Seen from the WAIC Chairman's Statement

*On July 17, 2026, the 2026 World Artificial Intelligence Conference and High-Level Meeting on Global AI Governance opened in Shanghai. The conference, themed "Intelligent Partners, Co-creating the Future," released a chairman's statement.*

The statement is rich in content — from innovation-driven development to risk prevention, from inclusive benefit to global governance. It sketches a systematic framework for AI development. As a startup focused on cognitive architecture and Humanities-AI Interaction, we read three parts most closely. They happen to resonate with the directions we have long been exploring.

Here are some of our reflections.

## People first — from principle to architecture

The statement explicitly advocates "adhering to people-first, AI for good," emphasizing that AI should reach thousands of households.

This points to a real problem. When AI moves from the laboratory into the real world, it must not only "be able to do things," but also "understand proportion." In our observation, AI's capability is already powerful enough. What is genuinely missing is understanding of "the person." A large model can generate a complete report in an instant, yet it will still say the wrong thing on an occasion that does not call for a joke. This is not a capability problem. It is a structural problem: the AI system lacks a scheduling mechanism that identifies the scenario and adjusts proportion.

This is precisely the direction we have long explored. The two-layer framework of "**Cognitive Architecture [IR-CA-007]** + **Cognitive Operating System [IR-CA-008]**" that we proposed in our white paper is essentially an attempt to build a layer of scenario awareness and behavioral constraint for AI — so that beyond "what it can do," it also knows "what it should not do" and "how to do it in which scenario."

Our view: "People first" cannot remain at the level of principle. It needs to be translated into engineerable architectural constraints. The conference listing it as a core governance principle makes us more certain — the next stage of AI competition will shift from "who is smarter" to "who understands people better."

## Data ownership — from platform monopoly to user autonomy

The statement further emphasizes "ensuring data is manageable, controllable, and traceable," and strengthening personal information protection.

This touches a fundamental question of the AI era. When AI is deeply embedded in people's lives, people's digital memory — conversation records, behavioral preferences, personal habits — is becoming a new kind of asset. To whom does this memory belong?

In our view, users should have full disposal rights over their personal data — viewable, exportable, deletable, migratable. This is the starting point for our design of the **AI Memory Chain [IR-MC-001]** architecture. In technical implementation, we explore client-side encryption and tiered authorization mechanisms to safeguard user data autonomy while accommodating the platform's statutory responsibilities in compliance auditing and security monitoring.

Our view: the next stop for data governance is not "the platform manages data well." It is "the user truly owns the data." This is what **Memory Sovereignty [IR-MS-006]** means in practice. The conference listing data security and personal information protection as important topics of global AI governance is consistent with our exploration of a "user-controllable, platform-compliant" data governance model. We judge this direction will become the standard configuration of AI infrastructure.

## Agent boundaries — from post-hoc constraint to architectural nativity

The statement also proposes strengthening safety bottom lines, preventing abuse and misuse, ensuring AI remains under human control, and clarifying the decision-making authority and behavioral boundaries of agents.

As AI agents move from "dialogue" to "execution" — calling APIs, operating software, even participating in decision-making — the definition of behavioral boundaries has become more urgent than ever.

In our recently published *Tripartite Model of AI Agent White Paper*, we proposed that a complete commercial AI agent consists of three parts — the **General Cognitive Base [IR-GCB-002]**, the **Human-Interaction Core [IR-HIC-003]**, and the **Private Knowledge Domain [IR-PKD-004]**. Among them, the Human-Interaction Core is responsible for establishing rules and constraints on the agent's behavior at the architectural level.

Our basic thinking is: behavioral boundaries should not be a patch added after the fact. They should be native to architectural design. This is what **First-Class Architectural Layer [IR-FCAL-006]** means — the Human-Interaction Core is not an optional add-on. It is a construction vertex on par with cognitive capability and business knowledge.

Our view: the risk of agent loss of control cannot be solved by "being careful when using it." Behavioral constraints must be embedded at the architectural level. This is our core judgment, and it is the fundamental reason we designed the Human-Interaction Core as an independent layer in the Tripartite Model. The conference's emphasis on agent behavioral boundaries makes us more certain of the necessity of this technical route.

## Final words

The statement contains much more — open innovation, inclusive mutual learning, global governance. Each part deserves the industry's serious consideration. The three parts above are what we, as a startup focused on "the relationship between AI and people," feel most deeply about and are actively exploring.

Consensus has formed at the industry level. The next questions are: How should the technical architecture be built? How should the infrastructure be laid? How should standards and norms be implemented? These questions require the whole industry to answer together.

We are willing to contribute our technical practice to this answer sheet.
