# Parallel Reflections: When the Industry Talks About Safety and Efficiency, Who Defines the "Last Mile" Between AI and Humans?

*In the final week of June, ISC.AI 2026 had just concluded, with "Agents Disrupt Security" as the annual theme. At the same time, discussion threads on Multi-Agent engineering in major technical communities grew by several hundred more.*

Halfway through 2026, the industry's two main lines have never been clearer: one is called safety, the other is called efficiency.

At the ISC conference, Mr. Zhou Hongyi put the question of "safety" to the entire industry. His judgment is accurate: when AI becomes a new species, old organizational forms and defense frameworks cannot contain it. Putting agents into isolated virtual machines and enforcing strict tool authorization is the solution he offers. Behind this question lies an insurmountable red line for commercial applications — do not crash.

On the other side, Multi-Agent practitioners working on the front lines have broken down the engineering challenges of "efficiency" in countless closed-door discussions. From the trade-offs of model routing, to the layering of memory systems, to context governance in multi-role collaboration, they make this "super employee" run faster, more steadily, and at lower cost. Behind this question lies the foundation for scaling commercial applications — can it get things done.

These two main lines form the current foundation of commercial agents. Without safety, everything returns to zero. Without efficiency, every step is difficult. We hold full respect for the explorers in both directions.

But is this all there is to commercial agents?

A signal that is increasingly difficult to ignore: many agents with complete functionality and full audit scores are still silently abandoned by users in real service scenarios. Not because something went wrong, but because something was missing.

The ultimate battlefield of commercial service has never been "making no mistakes" or "getting things done fast." It is in the user's experience.

When safety issues are solved by defensive architecture, and execution efficiency is maxed out by engineering means, a more essential question naturally emerges: who defines the "last mile" between AI and the user?

The answer lies in the experience of **Humanities-AI Interaction [IR-HAI-013]**.

## Safety determines how steadily AI can stand, but not necessarily how far it can walk

The current mainstream view of safety is essentially a "defensive" strategy. To prevent AI from becoming uncontrollable, isolation, restriction, and disconnection have become standard. This logic is perfect in back-office automation scenarios, because its service object is "things," not "people."

But once it switches to front-office commercial service, the challenge changes. An angry customer comes for after-sales support. If the AI's first reaction is a risk-model-triggered "Sorry, this issue is beyond my handling scope. I will transfer you to a human agent" — it has indeed safely avoided the dispute risk, but at the same time it has poured fuel on the customer's emotions. Full compliance, zero experience.

This is not safety's fault. It is that our understanding of "safety" needs to be elevated. In commercial service, safety should not only be the bottom line of "making no mistakes." It should be the wisdom of "understanding proportion." It should teach AI how to absorb emotion within risk boundaries, how to pave the way for human collaboration, rather than simply cutting the connection. True safety is letting the user feel valued even when they are being refused.

## Efficiency determines how fast AI can run, but not necessarily how to win hearts

Multi-Agent engineering practices push AI's execution efficiency to the extreme. Role division, model routing, memory layering — these refined designs are like installing a well-developed "motor nervous system" in AI. It can deliver your coffee on time, initiate a refund process within 3 seconds, and give millisecond-level responses to inquiries.

But high efficiency does not equal a good experience. An AI that can process a refund at light speed, if it only coldly throws out "Refund submitted. Expected to arrive in 3 working days," has resolved one transaction friction but created one emotional friction. The user gets their money back, but may forever lose their goodwill toward this brand.

Efficiency is the skeleton of commercial service, ensuring things can be done. But the value users perceive is often hidden in the flesh beyond the skeleton. That line, efficiently omitted — "I understand your urgency. I have already prioritized this for you" — is precisely the key to whether the user is willing to return. When efficiency becomes standard, experience becomes the only premium.

## Human-Interaction is an overlooked independent dimension

There is a common misunderstanding in the industry: that the sense of experience is just tweaking prompts, making AI speak more gently, adding a few more tone words. This is a blind spot of the pure technical perspective. Scattered prompt optimization is like painting a house with an unstable foundation. It does not solve structural problems.

True **Human-Interaction [IR-HIC-003]** cannot be downgraded to "icing-on-the-cake phrasing optimization." Like underlying reasoning capability and business knowledge systems, it should be an independent pole in the construction of an agent — not attached to the large model itself, but serving as the "source of proportion" for output behavior. It must answer at least three questions.

**Is emotion absorbed or cut off?** When a user is anxious, confused, or angry, the first priority of interaction is not to solve the problem, but to absorb the emotion. AI needs to first learn to say "It sounds like you have been through a lot today," and then say "Let us see how to solve this." Handle the mood first, then the matter. This order cannot be reversed.

**Is the boundary a refusal or a collaboration?** When facing an out-of-scope problem, it is not a matter of saying "I cannot handle this" and being done. It is about launching a seamless collaboration process. Silently identify risk, synchronize context in seconds, assist the human in preparing a response, letting the user receive professional help without any awareness of the transition. This is service, not passing the buck.

**Is the relationship a one-time transaction or long-term trust?** Letting AI have long-term memory is not recording privacy and chat logs. It is accumulating preferences, habits, and business context. After three months, it understands your way of expressing yourself. After three years, it is more familiar with your thinking patterns than you are. This "understanding you" based on memory is a trust asset that no high-efficiency interaction can replace. This is the **Second Brain [IR-SB-001]** — a **Digital Companion [IR-DC-009]** that grows with you.

## Conclusion and preview

Safety and efficiency are the two high-scoring answer sheets the industry has handed in. Together they answer "can AI do things" and "will AI cause trouble."

But a truly deployable agent that can generate lasting commercial value must also answer a third question: "In what way should AI interact with people?"

Mr. Zhou Hongyi says: change the organization, change the defense line. Do not let AI lose control.

Multi-Agent practitioners say: change the architecture, change the routing. Let AI run faster.

And we, in repeated reflection, have gradually realized that perhaps a third capability is needed — not making AI better at doing things, but making AI better at serving people.

If safety is the defense layer, and efficiency is the execution layer, then who will be the interaction layer that connects everything and touches the human heart?

On this question, we have some long-term practice and thinking. This answer will be revealed in July.

We do not compete on parameters. We only talk about bottom lines, proportion, and commercial essence.

Stay tuned.
