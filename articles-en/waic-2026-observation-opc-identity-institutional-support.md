# Parallel Reflections: WAIC 2026 Observation — OPC Has an "Identity," But How Far Is Institutional Support?

*WAIC 2026 did something valuable: for the first time, it set up an OPC (One-Person Company) zone, with 180 companies participating.*

"One person + AI tools = one company." This new form is no longer just a geek's toy. It has moved from the margins to the mainstream. When 180 OPCs appeared alongside Huawei and Baidu with independent identities, the signal the industry sent was clear and powerful: AI has dramatically lowered the barrier to entrepreneurship, allowing "one person" to become a complete production unit.

The conference gave OPC a "company" identity. This is the most important recognition.

But "company" is not just a label on a business license. Behind it stands an entire institutional support system.

Traditional companies endure across cycles because they have mature corporate governance, asset inventories, and inheritance mechanisms. But OPC's assets — client records, service processes, industry knowledge, configuration parameters — these core possessions of the digital age are now scattered in the pockets of various platforms. Boundaries are blurred. Ownership is unclear.

This is more like a typical time lag in industrial evolution: form innovation runs ahead of institutional adaptation.

It is worth acknowledging that the industry is already acting. On July 8, the group standard *Terminology for AI OPC* was officially released. Guangdong, Beijing, Hangzhou, Suzhou, and other regions have also issued targeted support policies. These efforts show that consensus is forming. But when we shift our gaze from "defining identity" to "operating assets," we find that the deep water of institutional support has only just surfaced.

Following this direction, three questions are especially worth the industry asking together. They correspond precisely to the full life cycle of a business entity — from "entry" to "continuity" to "exit."

**First, the inclusiveness of qualification channels.**

An OPC that wants to list an application and operate externally faces qualification thresholds tailored for traditional enterprises. For example, an ICP license requires a fixed number of employees and social security records. For a company with only one person, this means having to "raise" people just to get the license. An OPC is heavier than an "individual," yet lighter than a "company." In the existing system, there is no convenient channel for this "middle layer." This is not a call to lower the bottom line. It is a call for institutional innovation for a "lightweight company" — allowing OPCs to assume clear responsibilities while simplifying procedures.

**Second, the clarity of data ownership.**

When a company has only one person, the company's data assets are the individual's entire livelihood. Data is stored on Platform A. Rules are stored on Platform B. Client relationships live in a social app. Once a platform adjusts its API or restricts an account, the connection to clients may snap instantly. A client relationship accumulated over three years of OPC operation could vanish entirely because a platform account was reclaimed. This is not a technical problem. It is a problem of defining property rights. This data lacks both traceability and portability — allowing data to be traceable and to follow the person. It also lacks clear ownership definition — giving data assets clear boundaries and property rights. Although Suzhou, Guangzhou, and other regions have explored data intellectual property registration, there is still a considerable distance from forming a universal institutional arrangement. The "data" referred to here contains at least two layers: one is exportable records such as orders and reconciliations; the other is client preferences, trust, and repurchase habits — the latter is more valuable than the former, yet harder to take away.

**Third, the completeness of exit mechanisms.**

Traditional company deregistration has a liquidation process — a beginning and an end. When an OPC ceases operation, its brand reputation, service rules, and client trust — is there any possibility of inheritance, trading, or liquidation? If entry is free but exit cannot take away one's own digital assets, then the "one-person company" is not yet a sustainable business species. The ownership, portability, and inheritance of data are precisely the biggest blank spaces in the current system. Although many regions have implemented simplified deregistration and other exit facilitation measures, these measures target the deregistration of legal personality, not the inheritance and transfer of digital assets. The latter remains an institutional blind spot.

Beyond these institutional gaps, there is an easily overlooked operational risk — the "limited liability" of OPC may be more fragile in practice than imagined. Company law applies a reversal of burden of proof for one-person companies: if it cannot be proven that company property is independent from personal property, the shareholder bears joint liability for company debts. In the AI era, OPC receipts, settlements, and automatic deductions can easily bypass the corporate account. Once this line falls, the limited liability of a "one-person company" reverts to unlimited liability.

WAIC 2026 demonstrated the possibility of AI making "one person" stronger. The conference opened this door. The next agenda is how to pave the road: after "one person" becomes stronger, assets should still be autonomous, accumulable, and inheritable.

This question has no ready-made answer. But it deserves to be seriously raised — before AI OPCs grow from 180 to 1.8 million.
