# Parallel Reflections: When the Industry Guards Against the "Information Black Box," the AI Era Needs Checks and Balances

*Recently, two independent perspectives pointed to the same problem at the same time.*

A Silicon Valley investor observed: when an enterprise hands all its core data and processes to a single system for processing, it is like "giving information and execution power to the same person." This is extremely dangerous — a powerful minister can tamper in both directions during information transmission, and systems carry similar risks.

The CEO of Mistral warned: closed-source vendors forcing data retention is a huge trap. Once your enterprise data is seen through and taken, the other party gains enormous commercial leverage and can cross over into your industry.

Their arguments are not entirely the same, but they point to the same conclusion: you cannot hand information, decision-making, and execution all to the same black box.

This vigilance is correct.

The risk of excessive concentration of power does not depend on whether the person holding power is a "good person" or a "bad person." It depends on whether the institutional design itself has checks and balances.

But after vigilance, what then?

One voice says "master everything yourself." But most users do not have this capability — no technical team, no operations experience, no energy to understand the underlying logic. Telling them to "master everything" is beyond their capacity.

Another voice says "trust the platform." But business history has repeatedly proven that when a platform simultaneously controls your data, decisions, and execution chain, it has both the incentive and the ability to use these resources to enter your industry.

This is not "the vendor is a bad actor." It is "the structure makes power unbalanced."

Facing this structural risk, political history offers a mature approach — not trusting good people, but separating powers. Decision-making, execution, and supervision must be dispersed, checking each other. No single party should hold all power, nor should it hold all information.

The AI era also needs such checks and balances.

If we break down the operation of an AI agent, it involves three distinct elements:

**Cognitive capability** — understanding, reasoning, generation. This is what large model vendors excel at.

**Business knowledge** — industry data, service processes, product information. These can only be held by the merchant.

**Behavioral boundaries** — what should be said, what should not, and what should be done in which situations. This judgment comes from the long-term practical accumulation of frontline practitioners, and it is not in any code repository.

Three elements, each performing its own function, checking each other. No single entity should simultaneously control all three — not because they are "bad," but because structurally, power should not be overly concentrated.

The risk of the information black box is real. The solution is not "master everything yourself," nor is it "hand it to someone else." It is to use institutional design as a check: who controls what, who does not control what, and who can replace whom.

Beyond safety and efficiency, there is a third path — checks and balances, letting each element return to its proper place, irreplaceable and unmonopolizable.
