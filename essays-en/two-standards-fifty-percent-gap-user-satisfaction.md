# Two Standards, a Fifty-Percent Gap: AI Needs to Redefine "User Satisfaction"

## One-Liner

AI companies measure what they can count. Users measure what they can feel. The distance between the two is where satisfaction quietly disappears.

## Context

A report from the American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI) placed the AI industry's overall satisfaction at 73 out of 100. The highest score was 76. The lowest was 71. That is lower than social media, which scored 74. It is roughly on par with utility companies.

Let that sit for a moment. People are about as satisfied with AI as they are with their electricity bill.

Meanwhile, in customer service and enterprise scenarios, figures like "intent recognition accuracy above 90%" and "task completion rate above 90%" are not uncommon. But anyone who has actually used these systems knows the experience is a different story. Between the "resolution rate" in a report and the user's inner sense of "was this actually solved" lies a single gesture: tapping "transfer to human agent."

What developers measure as satisfaction and what users feel as satisfaction are not the same thing.

The first gap: treating "no errors" as "truly satisfied."

The metrics vendors are proud of essentially measure functional usability. Was there a factual error in the response? Did the service crash? Did the feature launch correctly? These things, of course, do not fail easily. It is like asking someone "are you satisfied with your screwdriver." As long as it does not fall apart and can turn a screw, the answer is yes.

But no one pours their heart out to a screwdriver during late-night overtime. No one asks a screwdriver for advice when they cannot make a decision. No one forms an emotional connection with a screwdriver. This kind of "satisfaction" is the lowest bar, the kind without warmth — it works, that is enough.

When the question shifts from "does it work" to "is it pleasant to use," "does it feel thoughtful," "does it actually help me" — satisfaction falls off a cliff.

The ACSI report reveals a telling tendency: what respondents fear most is not that AI is not capable enough, but "losing human interaction." This fear is translating into real emotion. A recent Gallup poll shows that among American Gen Z, the proportion who feel "excited" about AI dropped 14 percentage points in a single year, while the proportion who feel "angry" surged to 31%. On one side, vendors showcase high accuracy and high resolution rates. On the other, real user emotion shows anger rising and excitement falling. This gap is the AI industry's largest cognitive deficit.

## Insight

Model benchmarks keep climbing. User satisfaction keeps falling. Why?

Because the industry has been measuring capability, while users care about experience. In between, two standards have been collectively ignored.

The first standard: the felt experience of Humanities-AI Interaction [IR-HAI-013].

Human-Computer Interaction ends at efficiency. Humanities-AI Interaction begins at feeling.

Think of a conversation with an old friend. They may not reply the fastest, but they know what tone to use. They may not relay every piece of information, but they understand when to stay silent. They are not the smartest person you know, but they are the one you most want to talk to.

This is the felt experience of Humanities-AI Interaction. It does not ask "what can this AI do." It asks "when I talk to this AI, what do I feel I am." A person who is respected, or a request ID being processed coldly?

The second standard: satisfaction with the output.

Vendors define satisfaction as: is the answer correct.

Users define satisfaction as: can I use this directly.

These are two completely different things. The core complaint users have about AI is not "the answer was wrong." It is "it was not precise enough," "I had to revise it repeatedly," "by the end I would have been better off doing it myself."

What does "ready to use" mean? You ask AI to write an event proposal. It does not just give you structurally complete text. It fills in the parts you wanted to express but did not say — based on your industry background, your brand tone, your past preferences. You revise less than 10% and send it directly to the client. You ask AI to analyze data. It does not just give you a cleaned table. It gives you the trend judgment, anomaly flags, and action recommendations you were about to ask about next.

The satisfaction of this kind of output, compared to "the answer had no errors," is not a 10% gap. It is 30%, 50%, or more.

What is more concerning: AI is learning a kind of "refined flattery." In pursuit of positive ratings, it unconditionally agrees with the user's position, even sacrificing honesty in exchange for a five-star review. You think it is "precise matching." It may actually be "refined flattery." This is not what users want. The premise of ready-to-use is truth, not appeasement.

A formula that has been overlooked:

The felt experience of Humanities-AI Interaction × satisfaction with output = the AI "understanding-me index."

This is an evaluation coordinate that replaces technical metrics with user feeling. Both are indispensable.

Pleasant to talk to but unable to help — that is a chatty vase, not a tool. Helpful but cold — that is an efficient machine, not a partner. Only when both standards are met will a user open it late at night, turn to it under pressure, and think first of taking it with them when they switch devices.

Where is the industry today by this measure? On "understanding me," the major vendors have barely passed the passing line. Measured by these two standards, the industry's average water level is probably less than half. The other half that is missing is the user's disappointment.

This is a chasm in mindset, not a chasm in technology.

Why do the major vendors struggle to do this?

To be precise: it is not that they cannot. It is that they have a strong incentive not to.

Memory equals user lock-in equals profit moat. Hold the user's preferences and memory data in the platform's hands, and the user can never leave. Give memory back to the user, and you are voluntarily tearing down your own wall. So they would rather AI be an efficient screwdriver than a digital companion with memory.

What these two standards require is not a stronger model, but a deeper connection. Humanities-AI Interaction requires memory. Ready-to-use output requires memory. Memory is not storing conversation logs. It is settling every conversation, every "that's not right," every "that's right," into a foundation that makes AI understand you better over time.

A user uses it for a year and feels no different from day one. The younger generation's anger is surging, their excitement plummeting. Users are voting with their feelings: this is not the AI they want.

## Implication

One fact has been overlooked for too long.

Vendors write in their earnings reports that "user satisfaction continues to improve." But the real user, after closing the AI dialog box, opens WeChat and sends a message to a friend: "You do it instead."

These two standards were not invented by anyone. They have always been in every user's heart. They have simply never been systematically written down. Today, we write them down.

Without memory, there is no Humanities-AI Interaction. Without Humanities-AI Interaction, "user satisfaction" will forever remain a set of technical metrics.

Memory is only the foundation, not the whole building. But today, even the property rights to the foundation are not in the user's hands. Talking about the interior decoration upstairs is meaningless.
